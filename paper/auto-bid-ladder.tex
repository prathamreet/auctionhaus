% ─── ABSTRACT ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Every large online auction platform offers proxy bidding, where a bidder
registers a private valuation ceiling and the platform automatically places
counter-bids until that ceiling is reached. Despite its ubiquity, prior literature
does not formalize how platforms should concurrently resolve competing proxy agents
when a fresh manual bid arrives. Commercial platforms typically compute the clearing
price via closed-form second-price formulas and insert a single winning row,
discarding intermediate bids. While computationally efficient, this single-jump
approach destroys auditable log fidelity---vital for regulatory compliance and
shill bidding detection---and introduces race conditions with overlapping manual
bids. Conversely, naively invoking the bidding service per increment induces
nested-transaction hazards, ORM round-trip overhead, and deadlock cycles.

This paper presents the Atomic Ladder Protocol, which evaluates competing
proxy agents and commits the entire ascending bid sequence within a single
atomic database transaction. By enforcing a two-tier hierarchical row-locking
discipline (the auction row followed by participant wallets in ascending
lexicographical user ID order), the protocol guarantees serializability and
deadlock freedom. We formally prove four core properties: log fidelity,
Vickrey equivalence under discrete increments, serializability, and deadlock
freedom, alongside a closed-form iteration bound. We evaluate the protocol on
PostgreSQL~15 and Redis~7. At 100 concurrent virtual users, an event-sourced
Redis Stream sequencer sustains 975.0~bids/s ingress throughput (8.1~ms p95 latency)
and 182.4~bids/s durable persistence throughput, achieving a $\mathbf{6.1\times}$
improvement in durable commit throughput over direct row-locking while preserving
bit-exact log determinism.
\end{abstract}

Concurrency control, online auctions, proxy bidding, real-time systems,

% ─── I. INTRODUCTION ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

In an English ascending auction where participants have registered private valuation
ceilings, the arrival of a new manual bid requires the platform to resolve competing
proxy commitments and establish the prevailing clearing price. Let $M_i$ denote the
private ceiling of bidder~$i$ and $\delta$ the minimum bid increment. In discrete
English auctions, overcoming the runner-up's valuation requires advancing to the
smallest discrete increment strictly exceeding $M_{(2)}$:
$\lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta$, where $\lfloor x \rfloor_\delta = \lfloor x / \delta \rfloor \cdot \delta$
represents discrete grid projection. Classic auction theory~\cite{Vickrey1961}
establishes that under truthful delegation, the stable clearing price is
\begin{equation}
  P^\star = \min\!\bigl(M_{(1)},\; \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta\bigr)
  \label{eq:vickrey}
\end{equation}
where $M_{(1)} \geq M_{(2)} \geq \cdots$ orders ceilings descendingly. The bidder
with the highest ceiling wins and pays one increment above the runner-up's valuation,
preserving second-price equivalence.

Because~(\ref{eq:vickrey}) is closed-form, commercial auction platforms routinely
bypass stepwise ladder simulation: they evaluate the formula, insert a single winning
bid row at~$P^\star$, and broadcast the updated price. Empirical architectural studies
of production platforms (e.g., eBay~\cite{Roth2002,Shoup2004,Ockenfels2006}) confirm that
consumer-facing bid histories record discrete jumps to prevailing second-price increments
rather than the executed trajectory. While this \emph{single-jump} design minimizes
database write volume ($O(1)$ writes), it introduces a severe systemic deficiency:
the permanent loss of \emph{log fidelity}.

Log fidelity is a fundamental audit and security requirement. Regulatory oversight,
dispute resolution, and anti-fraud pipelines~\cite{Trevathan2007,Ford2010} (such as
shill bidding classifiers that analyze increment cadence, inter-arrival time distributions,
and escalation patterns) require the fine-grained sequence of intermediate offers to
reside durably within relational storage. Platforms encounter an architectural trilemma:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Single-Jump Evaluation:} High throughput ($O(1)$ writes) and zero
    internal lock contention, but total destruction of intermediate audit logs.
  \item \textbf{Naive Stepwise Chaining:} Invoking the bidding service once per increment.
    This pattern risks partial-commit anomalies (a crash or timeout midway leaves the
    database corrupted), incurs severe ORM round-trip latency, and causes cyclic
    deadlocks across concurrent wallet escrow updates.
  \item \textbf{The Atomic Ladder Protocol (Our Approach):} Within a single atomic database
    transaction, it evaluates candidate proxies, enforces multi-party escrow holds,
    and commits a complete, gap-free sequence of intermediate rungs while guaranteeing
    serializability and deadlock freedom.
\end{enumerate}

The core technical novelty of our work resolves the domain tension between dynamic
multi-agent game equilibria and relational database scheduling. Unlike generic database
transactions where read/write sets are static, proxy resolution operates over a dynamically
evolving candidate pool where participants drop out non-linearly based on private ceilings
and real-time wallet solvency. We synthesize economic ladder dynamics with a two-tier
global lock hierarchy and event-sourced sequencing. Specifically, we make three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Protocol Design.} We present the Atomic Ladder Protocol, an algorithm
    that dynamically evaluates candidate proxies, maintains multi-user escrow invariants
    ($W_{\text{winner}}.\text{held} \mathrel{-}= \text{price}$, $W_{\text{challenger}}.\text{held} \mathrel{+}= \text{next}$),
    and records each ladder increment within a single bounded transaction. A two-tier
    global lock order (auction row $\to$ lexicographically sorted wallet rows) rules
    out deadlock cycles across all concurrent platform writers.
  \item \textbf{Theoretical Foundations and Correctness.} We prove four core properties:
    (i)~\emph{Log Fidelity} (gap-free increment sequence), (ii)~\emph{Vickrey Equivalence}
    (convergence to exact second-price equilibrium $P^\star$), (iii)~\emph{Serializability},
    and (iv)~\emph{Deadlock Freedom}. Furthermore, we establish a closed-form iteration
    bound governed strictly by runner-up valuation $M_{(2)}$.
  \item \textbf{Dual Architecture and Empirical Evaluation.} We evaluate the protocol on
    \textsc{AuctionHaus} across direct row-locking (synchronous ACID durability) and an
    event-sourced Redis Stream sequencer (sustaining 975.0~bids/s ingress at 100 concurrent
    VUs and 182.4~bids/s durable persistence, a $6.1\times$ improvement over direct locking).
    We demonstrate bit-exact log determinism and analyze completion trade-offs.
\end{enumerate}

% ─── II. RELATED WORK ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work and Background}

\subsection{Auction Theory and Proxy Bidding}
Single-item auction theory originates with Vickrey~\cite{Vickrey1961}, establishing
truthful bidding as a weakly dominant strategy in second-price sealed-bid auctions.
Milgrom and Weber~\cite{Milgrom1982} generalized this to affiliated-value settings,
confirming revenue equivalence between ascending and second-price formats~\cite{Krishna2009}.
Roth and Ockenfels~\cite{Roth2002} and Ockenfels et al.~\cite{Ockenfels2006} analyzed
proxy bidding on eBay and Amazon, observing that strategic proxy submission mirrors
Vickrey's sealed-bid game. Production architectures~\cite{Shoup2004} routinely bypass
ladder simulation to optimize throughput. However, fraud detection systems~\cite{Trevathan2007,Ford2010}
rely on granular bid timing and increment sequences, rendering intermediate log preservation
critical.

\subsection{Transactional Concurrency and Lock Ordering}
Row-level pessimistic locking and serializability theory were formalized by Eswaran
et al.~\cite{Eswaran1976} and Bernstein et al.~\cite{Bernstein1987}. Gray and
Reuter~\cite{Gray1992} proved that acquiring resources in a global total order eliminates
deadlock cycles. In standard OLTP workloads (e.g., TPC-C payment profiles), transaction
read/write sets are known \emph{a priori} from request parameters. In proxy bidding,
however, the active participant set is dynamically determined by price escalation.
Our protocol resolves this by deriving the maximal candidate envelope
$U = \{u_{\text{trig}}\} \cup \{u_i \in \mathcal{P}\}$ and acquiring row locks in
lexicographical order upfront, reconciling dynamic game dynamics with static deadlock-freedom
invariants.

\subsection{High-Throughput Stream Architectures}
To alleviate database row-lock contention under terminal auction flurries, high-throughput
architectures leverage single-writer logs inspired by the LMAX Disruptor~\cite{Thompson2011}
and partitioned append logs such as Kafka~\cite{Kreps2011,Kleppmann2017} and Redis Streams.
While streaming engines provide sub-millisecond sequential ingestion, they trade off
immediate relational ACID guarantees. Helland~\cite{Helland2007} and Gilbert and
Lynch~\cite{Gilbert2002} examine log-based decomposition as an alternative to distributed
locking. Our dual architecture pairs an in-memory sequencer with a relational ladder worker,
reconciling sub-millisecond ingestion with durable log fidelity.

% ─── III. SYSTEM MODEL ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{System Model and Problem Statement}

\subsection{Objects and Operations}
The platform manages three durable relational entities:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Auction} $A$: carries start price, current price, increment $\delta$,
    status, and end time. One row per auction ID.
  \item \textbf{Wallet} $W_u$: stores $\{\text{balance},\,\text{heldAmount}\}$ per user $u$.
    Available balance is $\text{balance}_u - \text{heldAmount}_u$.
  \item \textbf{AutoBid} $\alpha_{u,A} = (u, A, M_{u,A})$: private delegation ceiling
    $M_{u,A}$ for user $u$ on auction $A$. At most one active per $(u,A)$ pair.
\end{itemize}

Two write operations update this state:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PlaceBid}$(u, A, p)$: manual bid $p$. Atomically verifies $p \geq \text{price}_A + \delta_A$
    and $\text{balance}_u - \text{held}_u \geq p$, releases previous winning hold
    ($W_{\text{prev}}.\text{held} \mathrel{-}= p_{\text{prev}}$), acquires hold on $W_u$
    ($W_u.\text{held} \mathrel{+}= p$), and sets $\text{price}_A \leftarrow p$.
  \item \textbf{ResolveAutoBids}$(A, u_{\text{trig}})$: triggered post-PlaceBid. Traverses
    the proxy pool, selects challengers in priority order, and emits one bid row per rung
    within a single transaction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concurrency Assumptions}
We assume: (1)~PostgreSQL default isolation (\texttt{READ COMMITTED}); (2)~\texttt{SELECT ... FOR UPDATE}
exclusively blocks concurrent updaters; (3)~multiple worker processes consume from a BullMQ queue,
with per-auction serialisation governed by database row locks; and (4)~ResolveAutoBids is idempotent
under job redelivery.

\subsection{Desired Properties}
For a trigger bid at price $p_0$ resolving over auto-bid pool $\mathcal{P}$ to clearing price $P^\star$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{P1 -- Log Fidelity:} Exactly one row committed at each price $p_0 + k\delta$
    for $k = 1, \ldots, K$ where $p_0 + K\delta = P^\star$.
  \item \textbf{P2 -- Vickrey Equivalence:} Final price equals $P^\star$, with winning bid
    owned by bidder with ceiling $M_{(1)}$.
  \item \textbf{P3 -- Serializability:} Interleaved execution with other writers is conflict-equivalent
    to a serial schedule.
  \item \textbf{P4 -- Deadlock Freedom:} Execution cannot deadlock against any writer obeying
    the global lock hierarchy.
  \item \textbf{P5 -- Bounded Work:} Executed rungs satisfy $K^\star = \lceil (P^\star - p_0)/\delta \rceil \leq \lceil (\lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta - p_0)/\delta \rceil$, halting immediately when the runner-up is priced out.
\end{itemize}

% ─── IV. THE ATOMIC LADDER PROTOCOL ─────────────────────────────────────────
\section{The Atomic Ladder Protocol}

\subsection{Lock-Order Invariant}
We enforce a strict two-tier global lock order across all platform transactions:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Acquire the auction row \texttt{FOR UPDATE} first, then acquire every participating
wallet \texttt{FOR UPDATE} in ascending \texttt{userId} lexicographic order.}
\end{quote}
All endpoints (PlaceBid, Withdraw, BuyNow, ConfirmPayment, EndAuction, ResolveAutoBids)
adhere to this sequence. Because the resource hierarchy is acyclic, wait-for cycles
cannot form (Property~P4).

\subsection{Pseudocode}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:ladder} details \texttt{ResolveAutoBids}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ResolveAutoBids$(A, u)$}
\label{alg:ladder}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{begin} transaction (\texttt{READ COMMITTED}); lock auction row $A$
\State load $A$: type, status, endTime, price, step $\delta$
\If{$A = \emptyset$ \textbf{or} status $\neq$ ACTIVE \textbf{or} now $>$ endTime \textbf{or} type $\neq$ ENGLISH}
  \State commit; \Return $\emptyset$
\EndIf
\State $P \gets$ active auto-bids on $A$ excluding $u$, sorted by max desc, createdAt asc
\If{$P = \emptyset$} \State commit; \Return $\emptyset$ \EndIf
\State $U \gets \text{sort}(\{u\} \cup \{u_i \mid \alpha_i \in P\})$; \ForAll{$u' \in U$} lock wallet $W_{u'}$ \EndFor
\State $b \gets$ latest WINNING bid by $u$ on $A$; price $\gets A.\text{price}$; winner $\gets b$
\State $P^\star \gets \min(M_{(1)}, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta)$; $K \gets \lceil (P^\star - \text{price})/\delta \rceil$; steps $\gets []$
\For{$k = 1$ \textbf{to} $K$}
  \State next $\gets$ price $+ \delta$; \If{next $> P^\star$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \State prune $\alpha_i \in P$ with $M_i <$ next
  \State $\alpha \gets \text{highest-max } \alpha_i \in P \text{ with } u_i \neq \text{winner.user}$
  \If{$\alpha = \emptyset$ \textbf{and} $P \neq \emptyset$ \textbf{and} winner.user $= P[0].\text{user}$ \textbf{and} next $\leq P^\star$}
    \State $\alpha \gets P[0]$ \Comment{Decisive rung to outbid runner-up valuation}
  \ElsIf{$\alpha = \emptyset$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \State $W \gets \text{wallet of } \alpha.\text{user}$; req $\gets (\alpha.\text{user} = \text{winner.user} \mathrel{?} \delta : \text{next})$
  \If{$W.\text{balance} - W.\text{held} < \text{req}$}
    \State deactivate $\alpha$; remove from $P$; \textbf{continue}
  \EndIf
  \State outbid winner; $W_{\text{winner}}.\text{held} \mathrel{-}= \text{price}$; $W.\text{held} \mathrel{+}= \text{next}$
  \State insert bid (user $=\alpha.\text{user}, A, \text{next}, \text{WINNING}, \text{auto})$; append to steps
  \State price $\gets$ next; winner $\gets$ new bid
\EndFor
\If{steps $\neq \emptyset$} update $A.\text{price} \gets \text{price}$ \EndIf
\State commit; \Return steps
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Implementation Notes}
The reference implementation is written in TypeScript using Prisma~5~\cite{Prisma2024}.
Pessimistic row locks are issued via \texttt{\$queryRaw} within an interactive transaction
callback, mapped to a single PostgreSQL \texttt{BEGIN/COMMIT} block. Upon commit, a single
\texttt{bid:ladder} WebSocket event delivers the \texttt{steps} array to connected clients,
collapsing $N$ socket round-trips into one (reducing frame transmission from 2{,}000 to 200
for a 10-rung ladder with 200 watchers).

% ─── V. CORRECTNESS ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Correctness}

\subsection{Property P1: Log Fidelity}
\begin{theorem}[Log Fidelity]
Let $p_0$ be the auction price post-trigger bid, and $P^\star$ the final price post-ResolveAutoBids.
Then for every $p \in \{p_0 + \delta, p_0 + 2\delta, \ldots, P^\star\}$, the database contains
exactly one row at price $p$, committed in price-ascending order.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:ladder} initializes $\text{price} = p_0$. Each non-skipped iteration
evaluates $\text{next} = \text{price} + \delta$, writes a row at $\text{next}$, and advances
$\text{price} \leftarrow \text{next}$. Skipped iterations (insolvent proxies) deactivate the
candidate without advancing $\text{price}$, preserving gap-free increments.
The loop terminates when no challenger covers $\text{next}$ or $\text{next} > P^\star$.
Since inserts occur within a single transaction, PostgreSQL assigns monotonic insertion-order
identifiers (\texttt{ctid}). The exclusive auction row lock blocks concurrent PlaceBid
operations, preventing external write interleaving.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Property P2: Vickrey Equivalence}
\begin{theorem}[Vickrey Equivalence]
For active auto-bids $\mathcal{P}$ on auction $A$ and manual trigger bid $p_0$, the final
clearing price equals $P^\star = \min(M_{(1)},\, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta)$,
with winning bidder $u_{(1)}$ holding ceiling $M_{(1)}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Order limits descendingly: $M_{(1)} \geq M_{(2)} \geq \cdots$, breaking ties deterministically
by registration timestamp. Trigger bid starts at $\text{cur} = p_0$. We proceed by induction on
rung $k \geq 1$ with price $\text{next} = p_0 + k\delta$.

\emph{Base case ($k=1$):} If $M_{(1)} < p_0 + \delta$, all proxies are pruned; price remains
$p_0 = P^\star$ with $u_{\text{trig}}$ winning. If $M_{(1)} \geq p_0 + \delta$, candidate
selection chooses $u_{(1)}$ to place the first rung. If $M_{(2)} < p_0 + \delta$, $P^\star = p_0 + \delta$;
the loop terminates on step 2 with $u_{(1)}$ winning at $P^\star$.

\emph{Inductive step:} Assume $\text{cur} = p_0 + k\delta < M_{(2)}$ with winner $w \in \{u_{(1)}, u_{(2)}\}$.
At $\text{next} = \text{cur} + \delta \leq M_{(2)} \leq M_{(1)}$, both proxies remain solvent and eligible.
Challenger selection excludes current winner $w$, choosing $\text{argmax}_{i \neq w} M_i$. If $w = u_{(1)}$,
it selects $u_{(2)}$; if $w = u_{(2)}$, it selects $u_{(1)}$. Any third proxy $u_{(j)}$ ($j \geq 3$)
is strictly dominated since $\min(M_{(1)}, M_{(2)}) \geq M_{(j)}$. The ladder strictly alternates
between $u_{(1)}$ and $u_{(2)}$.

\emph{Termination:} When $\text{next} > M_{(2)}$, $u_{(2)}$ and all lower proxies are pruned.
If $u_{(1)}$ won the prior rung at $\text{cur} \leq M_{(2)}$ and $\text{cur} < P^\star$,
line~18 selects $u_{(1)}$ to place the decisive rung at $P^\star$, outbidding $M_{(2)}$.
If $u_{(2)}$ won the prior rung, $u_{(1)}$ is selected as challenger to place the final bid at $P^\star$.
In both cases, the ladder halts at $P^\star = \min(M_{(1)}, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta)$
with $u_{(1)}$ winning.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Properties P3 and P4: Serializability and Deadlock Freedom}
\begin{theorem}[Serializability and Deadlock Freedom]
Any execution of ResolveAutoBids interleaved with concurrent platform writers is
conflict-serializable and deadlock-free.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every writer acquires the auction row lock prior to any wallet lock. Because the auction row
is the sole shared entity touched by all operations, the order of auction lock acquisition
establishes the conflict-equivalent serial schedule (Property~P3). Wallets are subsequently
acquired in ascending lexicographical \texttt{userId} order. By Gray and Reuter's
theorem~\cite{Gray1992}, total lock ordering eliminates wait-for cycles, ensuring deadlock
freedom (Property~P4).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Property P5: Bounded Work and Complexity}
The executed ladder length is bounded by runner-up valuation $M_{(2)}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  K^\star &= \left\lceil \frac{P^\star - p_0}{\delta} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{\min(M_{(1)},\, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta) - p_0}{\delta} \right\rceil \\
  &\leq \left\lceil \frac{\lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta - p_0}{\delta} \right\rceil
\end{split}
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
The ladder halts as soon as the runner-up is priced out, never escalating to $M_{(1)}$
unless $M_{(2)} \geq M_{(1)} - \delta$. Each rung performs $O(|\mathcal{P}|)$ in-memory
candidate filtering and $O(1)$ database writes (one bid row, two wallet updates).
Total transactional write volume is $O(K^\star)$ rows within a single commit.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We benchmark the protocol on PostgreSQL~15 and Redis~7 under workloads driven by k6~\cite{k6}.
Tests run for 15~seconds against an active English auction ($p_0 = \text{Rs.\,}1{,}000$,
$\delta = \text{Rs.\,}100$, wallet balances Rs.\,$10^8$). We compare two pipelines:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Direct (FOR UPDATE):} Synchronous persistence. The endpoint acquires the auction

  \item \textbf{Sequencer (Redis Stream):} Decoupled ingestion. Bids arrive at

    and return HTTP~202 immediately. A background consumer drains the stream via \texttt{XREADGROUP}
    and applies bids to PostgreSQL serially via the Atomic Ladder transaction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Throughput and Latency}
Table~\ref{tab:throughput} summarizes performance across concurrency levels $C \in \{1, 10, 100\}$.
Under low contention ($C=1$), sequencer ingress achieves 5.7~bids/s against 3.5~bids/s for direct
locking ($1.6\times$ difference). At $C=10$, direct latency rises to 1{,}284~ms (9.5~bids/s),
while sequencer ingress reaches 92.8~bids/s (48~ms p95) with consumer persistence committing 89.2~bids/s.

At $C=100$, direct row locking saturates the database connection pool: 120 of 570 requests abort
due to lock wait timeouts, yielding 30.0~bids/s at 5{,}772~ms p95 latency. In contrast, the Redis
Stream sequencer absorbs bursts at 975.0~bids/s with zero dropped requests at 8.1~ms p95 latency
($32.5\times$ ingress throughput gain, $712\times$ latency reduction). Crucially, evaluating
end-to-end durable persistence, the background worker achieves 182.4~bids/s commit throughput
(410~ms p95 latency)---a $\mathbf{6.1\times}$ throughput improvement over direct locking achieved
by eliminating lock contention.

\subsection{Determinism and Log Fidelity}
We evaluated reproducibility across five repeated executions ($K^\star = 8$). As shown in
Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}, SHA-256 hashes of the resulting bid logs confirm that the Atomic
Ladder is 100\% deterministic (1 hash), whereas recursive chaining exhibits partial-commit drift
(3--5 hashes). Furthermore, the Atomic Ladder achieves 100\% log fidelity (8/8 rows), whereas
single-jump retains only 12.5\% (1/8 rows) and recursive chaining averages 78.8\%.

% ─── VII. DISCUSSION ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Single-Jump Trade-offs and Anti-Sniping}
Single-jump evaluation remains appropriate for internal high-frequency matching engines
where intermediate bids are unobservable. For consumer auction platforms with live public
histories, log fidelity is mandatory. To accommodate anti-sniping extensions, our protocol
extends the auction deadline once per triggering manual bid, treating the ladder as an
atomic consequence of a single human action. Under BullMQ job redelivery, idempotency is
guaranteed: re-evaluating an already resolved ladder finds current price exceeding ceilings,
safely returning an empty step set without duplicate writes.

\subsection{Multi-Node Distributed Architecture}
\label{subsec:multinode}
In horizontally partitioned architectures, the protocol scales across two dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Distributed Relational Transactions (2PC Consensus):} When auction $A$ and
    wallets $\{W_u\}$ reside across multiple database shards (e.g., Citus or CockroachDB),
    ResolveAutoBids executes via Two-Phase Commit over Raft/Paxos consensus groups. Because our
    two-tier lock hierarchy is universal, distributed deadlocks remain mathematically impossible.
    While 2PC consensus incurs a $2\times\text{RTT}$ latency penalty, inlining the ladder
    resolution within a database stored procedure eliminates client-database network round-trips
    during inner loop iteration.
  \item \textbf{Partitioned Stream Sequencing:} Partitioning broker streams by
    $\text{hash}(\text{auctionId})$ routes all bids and ladder triggers for auction $A$ to a
    single partition leader. A dedicated partition worker serializes updates against the
    corresponding database shard, eliminating row-lock contention entirely while scaling
    linearly across independent auctions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
Measurements were conducted on a single host. In clustered environments with network-separated
nodes, physical RTT would increase absolute latencies. However, because synchronous lock contention
amplifies super-linearly with latency~\cite{Bernstein1987} while stream queuing remains additive,
the relative advantage of the decoupled sequencer over direct locking will be even greater in
distributed deployments.

% ─── VIII. CONCLUSION ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

The Atomic Ladder Protocol resolves competing proxy bids within a single atomic database
transaction, ensuring complete log fidelity, second-price Vickrey equivalence, serializability,
and deadlock freedom. Formal proofs establish correctness under arbitrary arrival orders and
derive a closed-form iteration bound. Evaluated on PostgreSQL and Redis, our event-sourced
sequencer achieves 975.0~bids/s ingress throughput and 182.4~bids/s durable persistence throughput
($6.1\times$ higher than direct locking) with 100\% determinism. Future work includes multi-auction
shared-wallet allocation, TLA$^+$ formal specification~\cite{Lamport2002}, and empirical benchmarking
across distributed database clusters.
