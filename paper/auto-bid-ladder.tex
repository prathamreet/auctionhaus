% ─── ABSTRACT ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Every large online auction platform offers proxy bidding, where a bidder
registers a private maximum and the system keeps placing small
counter-bids for them until that maximum is hit. Even though the feature
is everywhere, no published work says how a platform should resolve
competing proxy agents when a fresh manual bid comes in. What platforms
usually do is work out the final price with a formula and write one
database row for it, throwing away the back-and-forth in between. That is
fast, but it gives up two things operators actually need: a faithful
step-by-step record that can be used for disputes and fraud analysis, and
correct ordering when manual bidders and proxy resolution overlap. The
obvious alternative, calling the bid service once per increment, runs
into nested-transaction problems with common object-relational mappers
and can leave the auction half-updated if it fails partway.

This paper presents the Atomic Ladder Protocol, which writes every step
of a proxy-bid ladder as its own bid row inside one database transaction.
Locks are taken in two tiers, the auction row first and then the wallets
in user-id order, which keeps every concurrent writer free of deadlocks.
We set out five properties we want, prove three of them (log fidelity,
Vickrey-equivalent pricing, and deadlock freedom), and benchmark the
protocol on PostgreSQL~15 with Redis~7. At 100 concurrent bidders the
Redis Stream version holds 975~bids/s at a 95th-percentile latency of
8.1~ms, while direct row locking falls to 30~bids/s at 5{,}772~ms, a
$\mathbf{32.5\times}$ throughput gain.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Concurrency control, online auctions, proxy bidding, real-time systems,
serializability, two-phase locking, Vickrey auction.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

% ─── I. INTRODUCTION ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

Take an English auction where several people have each set a private
ceiling. When a new manual bid arrives, the platform has to decide how
many of those ceilings to act on and what price to settle at. Write
$M_i$ for the ceiling of bidder~$i$ and $\delta$ for the minimum
increment. In discrete English ascending auctions, defeating the runner-up's
valuation requires reaching the smallest grid increment strictly exceeding $M_{(2)}$:
$\lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta$, where $\lfloor x \rfloor_\delta = \lfloor x / \delta \rfloor \cdot \delta$
denotes the discrete grid projection. Standard auction theory~\cite{Vickrey1961}
establishes that, when participants delegate truthfully, the stable clearing price is
\begin{equation}
  P^\star = \min\!\bigl(M_{(1)},\; \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta\bigr)
  \label{eq:vickrey}
\end{equation}
where $M_{(1)} \geq M_{(2)} \geq \cdots$ orders the ceilings from
highest down (simplifying to $\min(M_{(1)}, M_{(2)} + \delta)$ when ceilings lie on
the increment grid). The bidder with the highest ceiling wins and pays one
increment above the runner-up valuation, matching the second-price sealed-bid outcome.

Since~(\ref{eq:vickrey}) is closed-form, commercial auction platforms
routinely bypass ladder simulation: they evaluate the formula, insert a single
winning bid row at~$P^\star$, and broadcast the updated price. Architectural
analyses and empirical studies of production auction platforms (e.g., eBay~\cite{Roth2002,Shoup2008,Ockenfels2006})
confirm that consumer-facing bid histories record only discrete jumps to
prevailing second-price increments rather than the fully executed ascending trajectory.
While this \emph{single-jump} design minimizes database write
amplification ($O(1)$ writes), it introduces a severe systemic deficiency:
the permanent loss of \emph{log fidelity}.

Log fidelity is not merely an aesthetic display preference; it is a fundamental
audit and compliance requirement. Regulatory oversight, dispute resolution,
and anti-fraud ML pipelines~\cite{Trevathan2007,Ford2010} (such as shill bidding
detectors that examine bid increment cadence, inter-arrival time distributions,
and tactical escalation patterns) require the complete, fine-grained sequence
of intermediate offers to reside durably within the relational database.

Faced with this limitation, platforms encounter an architectural trilemma:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Single-Jump Evaluation:} High throughput ($O(1)$ writes) and zero
    contention, but total destruction of intermediate log fidelity and auditability.
  \item \textbf{Naive Stepwise/Recursive Chaining:} Re-invoking the bid placement
    service once per increment. This pattern creates severe partial-commit hazards
    (a process crash or transient timeout at rung 7 of a 12-rung sequence leaves
    the database in an unrecoverable corrupted state), incurs high latency overhead
    from repeated ORM round-trips, and creates cyclic-wait deadlocks across
    concurrently updated user wallet escrows.
  \item \textbf{The Atomic Ladder Protocol (Our Approach):} Bridges economic
    game theory and ACID transaction processing. Within a single atomic database
    transaction, it evaluates the proxy pool, enforces multi-party escrow holds,
    and commits a complete, gap-free sequence of intermediate rungs while
    guaranteeing serializability and deadlock freedom.
\end{enumerate}

The core technical novelty of our work does not lie in transactional locking in the
abstract, but in resolving the specific domain tension between multi-party
economic equilibria and concurrent database scheduling. Unlike generic database
transactions where read/write sets are static, proxy resolution operates over a
dynamically evolving candidate pool where participants drop out based on private
valuation limits and real-time wallet solvency. We synthesize economic ladder
dynamics with a two-tier global lock hierarchy and event-sourced sequencing.
Specifically, we make three main contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Protocol Design.} We present the Atomic Ladder Protocol, an
    algorithm that dynamically prunes candidate proxies, maintains multi-user
    escrow invariants ($W_{\text{winner}}.\text{held} \mathrel{-}= \text{price}$,
    $W_{\text{challenger}}.\text{held} \mathrel{+}= \text{next}$), and records
    each ladder increment within a single bounded PostgreSQL transaction. A two-tier
    global lock order (auction row $\to$ lexicographically sorted wallet rows)
    rules out deadlock cycles across all concurrent platform writers.

  \item \textbf{Theoretical Foundations and Correctness.} We provide formal
    proofs for four core properties: (i)~\emph{Log Fidelity} (the database contains
    a strictly contiguous, gap-free increment sequence), (ii)~\emph{Vickrey Equivalence}
    (the ladder provably converges to the exact second-price equilibrium $P^\star$),
    (iii)~\emph{Serializability} (conflict equivalence to a serial schedule), and
    (iv)~\emph{Deadlock Freedom}. Furthermore, we establish a tight iteration bound
    governed strictly by the runner-up valuation $M_{(2)}$.

  \item \textbf{Dual Architecture and Empirical Evaluation.} We evaluate the
    protocol on the open-source \textsc{AuctionHaus} platform across two operational
    modes: direct row-locking (for synchronous ACID durability) and an event-sourced
    Redis Stream sequencer (for decoupled high-throughput ingestion sustaining
    975.0~bids/s at 100 concurrent VUs). We report reproducibility, log determinism
    hashes, and explicit completion trade-offs.
\end{enumerate}

The protocol runs in the open-source \textsc{AuctionHaus} platform. The
reference implementation is approximately 210~lines of TypeScript, packaged as a
BullMQ worker job that fires after every manual bid.

% ─── II. LITERATURE SURVEY ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Literature Survey}

This section covers the wider background our work builds on: the
economics of single-item auctions, the serializability theory behind
transactional databases, and the work on partitioned ordered logs that
our evaluation pipeline borrows from.

\subsection{Auction-Theoretic Foundations}

Single-item auction theory starts with Vickrey~\cite{Vickrey1961}, who
showed that with a second-price sealed-bid mechanism, bidding truthfully
is a weakly dominant strategy. Milgrom and Weber~\cite{Milgrom1982}
extended this to a broad range of independent-private-value and
affiliated-value settings and established the revenue equivalence between
ascending and second-price formats, which is what makes proxy bidding a
sensible platform feature in the first place. Krishna~\cite{Krishna2009}
pulls these threads together in a textbook and gives the formal tools we
use when we argue about the Vickrey equivalence of the atomic ladder.

\subsection{Concurrency and Distributed Systems Theory}

The serializability theory behind our correctness argument comes from
Eswaran, Gray, Lorie, and Traiger~\cite{Eswaran1976}, who introduced
predicate locks and the conflict-serializability criterion that two-phase
locking achieves. Lamport's~\cite{Lamport1978} happens-before relation
captures the logical order in which we commit ladder rungs. For
distributed deployments, the CAP result of Gilbert and
Lynch~\cite{Gilbert2002} sets out the consistency-versus-availability
trade-off that pushes us towards a single-leader stream serialiser rather
than multi-master writes. Helland~\cite{Helland2007} makes the case for
asynchronous, log-based decomposition as a practical substitute for
distributed transactions, and the Redis Stream sequencer we evaluate here
is exactly that kind of decomposition.

\subsection{Stream Architectures for Ordered Processing}

Using a partitioned, append-only log to serialise high-volume updates was
made popular by Kafka~\cite{Kreps2011}, and the same idea, in different
forms, sits behind Amazon Kinesis, Google Pub/Sub, and Redis Streams. Its
per-partition total order, durable replay, and at-least-once delivery
carry over to the bid-resolution problem without any change, which is why
our reference implementation uses it.

% ─── III. RELATED WORK ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Proxy Bidding Mechanisms}

The strategic side of proxy bidding goes back to
Vickrey's~\cite{Vickrey1961} study of second-price sealed tenders. More
recently, Roth and Ockenfels~\cite{Roth2002} looked at the proxy
mechanisms on eBay and Amazon and showed that submitting your true
ceiling is a weakly dominant strategy, which makes a proxy-driven English
auction strategically the same as Vickrey's sealed-bid game. Their focus
is on how bidders behave, though, not on how the platform resolves the
proxy pool inside. That implementation gap is what we deal with.

\subsection{Concurrency Control in Multi-Party Transaction Systems}

Row-level pessimistic locking is an established paradigm for serializing
conflicting writes, formalized by Bernstein, Hadzilacos, and
Goodman~\cite{Bernstein1987}. Taking locks in a global, total order prevents
deadlock cycles (Gray and Reuter~\cite{Gray1992}). In standard transaction
benchmarks (e.g., TPC-C payment profiles or paired bank transfers), the
read/write set is fixed and known \emph{a priori} from request parameters
(e.g., source and destination account IDs).

In contrast, concurrent proxy bidding exhibits a distinctive domain-specific
challenge: the exact set of active participants is \emph{dynamically determined}
by the price trajectory itself. As the ladder ascends, proxies drop out
non-linearly as their ceilings ($M_i$) or available wallet balances are exceeded.
Acquiring wallet locks dynamically as challengers emerge risks cyclic wait-for
dependencies against concurrent manual bidders or settlement processes. Our
protocol addresses this by deriving the maximal candidate envelope
$U = \{u_{\text{trig}}\} \cup \{u_i \in \mathcal{P}\}$ and acquiring row locks in
lexicographical order upfront, effectively reconciling dynamic multi-agent game
dynamics with static deadlock-freedom invariants.

\subsection{High-Throughput Sequencers and Decoupled Ingestion}

To bypass database row-lock contention under terminal auction flurries,
architectures often turn to single-writer logs inspired by the LMAX
Disruptor~\cite{Thompson2011} and partitioned append logs such as
Kafka~\cite{Kreps2011,Kleppmann2017} and Redis Streams. While these streaming engines excel at
high-speed sequential ingestion, they deliberately sacrifice immediate ACID
guarantees and relational queryability. Prior auction literature either assumes
centralized in-memory matching with delayed checkpointing or falls back to
relational single-jump updates. Our work demonstrates how an in-memory sequencer
can be cleanly paired with a relational Atomic Ladder worker to preserve both
sub-millisecond ingress latency and durable, relational log fidelity.

% ─── IV. SYSTEM MODEL ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{System Model and Problem Statement}

\subsection{Objects and Operations}

The platform exposes three durable objects:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Auction} $A$ carries five fields: start price,
    current price, minimum increment, status, and end time.
    A single row per auction ID.

  \item \textbf{Wallet} $W_u$ per user $u$ with
    $\{\text{balance},\,\text{heldAmount}\}$, representing total deposited
    funds and active escrow holds, respectively. The available (spendable)
    balance of $u$ is $\text{balance}_u - \text{heldAmount}_u$.

  \item \textbf{AutoBid} $\alpha_{u,A} = (u, A, M_{u,A})$: user $u$ has
    delegated up to $M_{u,A}$ for auction $A$. At most one per
    $(u,A)$ pair.
\end{itemize}

The platform exposes two write operations on this state:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PlaceBid}$(u, A, p)$: user $u$ manually bids amount $p$
    on auction $A$. Atomically validates $p \geq \text{currentPrice}_A +
    \delta_A$ and $\text{balance}_u - \text{heldAmount}_u \geq p$, releases
    the previous winning hold ($W_{\text{prev}}.\text{held} \mathrel{-}= p_{\text{prev}}$),
    places an escrow hold of $p$ on $W_u$ ($W_u.\text{held} \mathrel{+}= p$),
    and updates $\text{currentPrice}_A \leftarrow p$.

  \item \textbf{ResolveAutoBids}$(A, u_{\text{trig}})$: triggered after
    every successful PlaceBid. Walks the auto-bid pool, choosing
    challengers in priority order, and emits one bid row per ladder rung.
    This is the operation the protocol governs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concurrency Assumptions}

We assume:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item PostgreSQL default isolation level (\texttt{READ COMMITTED}).
  \item \texttt{SELECT \ldots FOR UPDATE} blocks concurrent updaters of
    the locked row.
  \item Multiple worker processes consume from the same BullMQ
    \texttt{auto-bid} queue; per-auction serialisation comes from the
    database lock, not the queue's group-key feature.
  \item BullMQ may re-deliver any job (worker crash, stalled-job
    recovery), so ResolveAutoBids must be idempotent.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Desired Properties}

For a manual bid that triggers a ladder resolution starting at price
$p_0$ and ending at $P^\star$ (as defined by
Equation~\ref{eq:vickrey} over the current auto-bid pool $\mathcal{P}$):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{P1 -- Log Fidelity.} The bid log contains, in commit
    order, exactly one row at each price
    $p_0 + k\delta$ for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, K$ where $p_0 + K\delta =
    P^\star$.

  \item \textbf{P2 -- Vickrey Equivalence.} The final price after the
    ladder commits equals $P^\star$, and the winning row is owned by
    the bidder with limit $M_{(1)}$.

  \item \textbf{P3 -- Serializability.} Any execution interleaved with
    other writers (PlaceBid, withdraw, settlement) is equivalent to
    some serial execution.

  \item \textbf{P4 -- Deadlock Freedom.} The protocol cannot deadlock
    against any other writer that obeys the global lock order.

  \item \textbf{P5 -- Bounded Work.} The exact number of executed rungs is
    strictly determined by the discrete clearing price:
    $K^\star = \lceil (P^\star - p_0) / \delta \rceil = \lceil (\min(M_{(1)}, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta) - p_0) / \delta \rceil \leq \lceil (\lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta - p_0) / \delta \rceil$,
    halting as soon as the runner-up is priced out rather than escalating to $M_{(1)}$.
\end{itemize}

% ─── V. PROTOCOL ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{The Atomic Ladder Protocol}

\subsection{Lock-Order Invariant}

We declare a single global lock order shared by every write operation in
the system:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Acquire the auction row \texttt{FOR UPDATE} first, then every
participating wallet \texttt{FOR UPDATE} in ascending \texttt{userId}
lexicographic order.}
\end{quote}

PlaceBid, Withdraw, BuyNow, ConfirmPayment, EndAuction, and
ResolveAutoBids all follow this same order. As long as every writer
sticks to it, the classical lock-ordering theorem applies and no cycle
can form in the wait-for graph (Property~P4).

\subsection{Pseudocode}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ladder} gives the procedure,
\texttt{ResolveAutoBids}$(A, u_{\text{trig}})$, where $u_{\text{trig}}$
is the user whose manual bid set off the resolution.

\begin{algorithm}[!htb]
\caption{ResolveAutoBids$(A, u)$}
\label{alg:ladder}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State begin transaction (READ COMMITTED)
\State lock auction row $A$
\State load $A$: type, status, endTime, price, step $\delta$
\If{$A$ missing or status $\neq$ ACTIVE}
  \State commit; return $\emptyset$
\EndIf
\If{now $>$ endTime} \Comment{Phase E4}
  \State commit; return $\emptyset$
\EndIf
\If{type $\neq$ ENGLISH}
  \State commit; return $\emptyset$
\EndIf
\State $P \gets$ active auto-bids on $A$, excluding $u$
\State sort $P$ by max desc, createdAt asc
\If{$P$ empty}
  \State commit; return $\emptyset$
\EndIf
\State $U \gets$ sorted ids $\{u\} \cup \{u_i$ in $P\}$
\ForAll{$u' \in U$}
  \State lock wallet row of $u'$
\EndFor
\State $b \gets$ latest WINNING bid by $u$ on $A$
\State price $\gets$ $A$.price; winner $\gets b$
\State $P^\star \gets \min(M_{(1)}, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta)$
\State $K \gets \lceil (P^\star - \text{price})/\delta \rceil$
\State steps $\gets$ empty list
\For{$k = 1$ to $K$}
  \State next $\gets$ price $+ \delta$
  \If{next $> P^\star$} \State break \EndIf
  \State drop from $P$ any $\alpha_i$ with $M_i <$ next
  \State $\alpha \gets$ highest-max $\alpha_i \in P$ with $u_i \neq$ winner.user
  \If{no such $\alpha$ \textbf{and} $P \neq \emptyset$ \textbf{and} winner.user $= P[0]$.user \textbf{and} next $\leq P^\star$}
    \State $\alpha \gets P[0]$ \Comment{Decisive rung to outbid runner-up valuation}
  \ElsIf{no such $\alpha$}
    \State break
  \EndIf
  \State $W \gets$ wallet of $\alpha$.user
  \State req $\gets$ (\textbf{if} $\alpha$.user $=$ winner.user \textbf{then} $\delta$ \textbf{else} next)
  \If{$W$.balance $- W$.held $<$ req}
    \State deactivate $\alpha$; remove from $P$; continue
  \EndIf
  \State outbid winner; $W_{\text{winner}}$.held $\mathrel{-}=$ price
  \State insert bid (user $=\alpha$.user, $A$, next, WINNING, auto)
  \State $W$.held $\mathrel{+}=$ next
  \State append bid to steps
  \State price $\gets$ next; winner $\gets$ new bid
\EndFor
\If{steps non-empty}
  \State update $A$.price $\gets$ price
\EndIf
\State commit; return steps
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Implementation Notes}

The reference implementation is written in TypeScript on Prisma\,5. The
\texttt{SELECT \ldots FOR UPDATE} statements go through Prisma's
\texttt{\$queryRaw}, because the generated client has no built-in syntax
for row locks. The outer transaction uses
\texttt{prisma.\$transaction(\textit{interactive callback})}, which
becomes a single PostgreSQL \texttt{BEGIN/COMMIT} pair.

Once the transaction commits, the worker sends a single
\texttt{bid:ladder} Socket.io event carrying the whole \texttt{steps}
array. It is one event for the ladder, not one per rung, and clients
animate the rungs from the array (see Section~\ref{sec:eval}). Sending
one event instead of $N$ turns $N$ socket round-trips into one for an
$N$-rung ladder, and this matters more than we expected at high fan-out:
a busy auction with 200 subscribed sockets and a 10-rung ladder goes from
2000 socket frames down to 200.

% ─── VI. CORRECTNESS ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Correctness}

\subsection{Property P1: Log Fidelity}

\begin{theorem}[Log Fidelity]
Let $p_0$ be the auction current price after the trigger manual bid
commits, and let $P^\star$ be the final price after ResolveAutoBids
returns. Then for every $p \in \{p_0 + \delta, p_0 + 2\delta, \ldots,
P^\star\}$, the bid log contains exactly one row at price $p$, and the
rows are committed in price-ascending order.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The for-loop in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ladder} starts at $\text{cur} = p_0$
and, on each non-skipped iteration, inserts a row at
$\text{next} = \text{cur} + \delta$ and assigns
$\text{cur} \leftarrow \text{next}$. Therefore the inserted prices form a
contiguous sequence $p_0 + \delta, p_0 + 2\delta, \ldots, p_0 +
K\delta$ where $K$ is the number of successful iterations.

If an iteration skips (affordability check fails on $\alpha^\ast$),
$\text{cur}$ is not advanced and $\alpha^\ast$ is removed from $\mathcal{P}$.
The next iteration retries with a smaller pool; the value
$\text{next} = \text{cur} + \delta$ is unchanged. So a skip does not
create a gap in the price sequence.

Termination: the loop breaks when no eligible challenger exists, i.e.\
every remaining $\alpha \in \mathcal{P}$ either has
$M_i < \text{next}$ (pruned by the limit filter) or is the current
winner (skipped by challenger selection). Since entries are only removed
when their limit is exceeded or affordability fails (never re-added),
$|\mathcal{P}|$ is non-increasing and the loop terminates in at most
$K_{\max}$ iterations (Property~P5). At termination, $\text{cur}$ holds
the final auction price and the bid sequence remains gap-free.

Commit order: all rows are inserted in the same transaction. Within a
PostgreSQL transaction, inserts are visible to subsequent statements in
the same transaction in the order they were issued; on commit, they
become visible to outside readers atomically but with insertion-order
identifiers (\texttt{ctid} ordering). Other transactions reading the bid
log after commit see the rows in price-ascending order.
Isolation from concurrent writers follows directly from the auction row
\texttt{FOR UPDATE} lock (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ladder}, line~2), which blocks any
concurrent \textbf{PlaceBid} on the same auction until the ladder transaction
commits; no external writer can insert an interleaved bid row into the sequence.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Property P2: Vickrey Equivalence}

\begin{theorem}[Vickrey Equivalence]
For any number of concurrent active auto-bids $\mathcal{P}$ on auction
$A$, after a manual bid by $u_{\text{trig}}$ at price $p_0$, the final
auction price after ResolveAutoBids commits equals
$P^\star = \min(M_{(1)},\, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta)$, where $M_{(1)}, M_{(2)}$
are the two largest limits among
$\mathcal{P} \cup \{p_0\}$ (treating the manual bid's effective limit as
$p_0$).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let the limits of participants in $\mathcal{P} \cup \{p_0\}$ be ordered as $M_{(1)} \geq M_{(2)} \geq \cdots \geq M_{(m)}$, where ties are broken deterministically by earlier registration timestamp. Bidder $u_{(1)}$ holds limit $M_{(1)}$, and $u_{(2)}$ holds $M_{(2)}$. The trigger bid begins with current price $\text{cur} = p_0$ held by $u_{\text{trig}}$ (whose effective ceiling in this round is $p_0$).

We trace candidate selection in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ladder} by induction on the ladder rungs $k \geq 1$, where each successful rung advances the price to $\text{next} = p_0 + k\delta$.

\emph{Base case ($k=1$):} If $M_{(1)} < p_0 + \delta$, no auto-bid covers the first increment; all $\alpha \in \mathcal{P}$ are pruned by the limit filter, the loop breaks, and the price remains $p_0 = P^\star$ with $u_{\text{trig}}$ winning. If $M_{(1)} \geq p_0 + \delta$, candidate selection picks $\alpha \in \mathcal{P} \setminus \{u_{\text{trig}}\}$ with maximal limit, which is $u_{(1)}$. Thus $u_{(1)}$ places the first ladder rung at $p_0 + \delta$ and becomes the current winner. If $M_{(2)} < p_0 + \delta$ (no runner-up covers the first rung), $\lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta \leq p_0$, so $\lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta \leq p_0 + \delta$; since $u_{(1)}$ is already at $p_0 + \delta = P^\star$, the loop terminates on step $k=2$ with $u_{(1)}$ winning at $P^\star$.

\emph{Inductive step:} Suppose the price has reached $\text{cur} = p_0 + k\delta < M_{(2)}$, with current winner $w \in \{u_{(1)}, u_{(2)}\}$. We assume participants maintain solvent wallet balances (guaranteed by the pre-ladder wallet verification; if a proxy's balance is exhausted, it is deactivated and pruned from $\mathcal{P}$, with induction continuing on the remaining solvent pool). At the next step, $\text{next} = \text{cur} + \delta \leq M_{(2)} \leq M_{(1)}$. Both $u_{(1)}$ and $u_{(2)}$ satisfy $M_i \geq \text{next}$ and remain in $\mathcal{P}$. The challenger selection picks the maximal candidate in $\mathcal{P}$ distinct from $w$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $w = u_{(1)}$, challenger selection excludes $u_{(1)}$ and picks $\text{argmax}_{i \neq (1)} M_i = u_{(2)}$ (since $M_{(2)} \geq M_{(j)}$ for all $j \geq 3$). Thus $u_{(2)}$ outbids $u_{(1)}$ at $\text{next}$.
  \item If $w = u_{(2)}$, challenger selection excludes $u_{(2)}$ and picks $u_{(1)}$ (since $M_{(1)} \geq M_i$ for all $i$). Thus $u_{(1)}$ outbids $u_{(2)}$ at $\text{next}$.
\end{itemize}
Any third proxy $u_{(j)}$ ($j \geq 3$) is never selected because at every step the non-winning member of $\{u_{(1)}, u_{(2)}\}$ has limit strictly greater than or equal to $M_{(j)}$. Hence, the ladder strictly alternates between $u_{(1)}$ and $u_{(2)}$, monotonically increasing by $\delta$ on each rung while $\text{next} \leq M_{(2)}$.

\emph{Termination:} The loop reaches a price $\text{cur}$ such that $\text{next} = \text{cur} + \delta > M_{(2)}$. At this rung, $u_{(2)}$ (and all lower-limit proxies) fail $M_i \geq \text{next}$ and are dropped from $\mathcal{P}$. Two cases arise depending on grid parity:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $u_{(1)}$ placed the bid at $\text{cur} \leq M_{(2)}$, then $u_{(1)}$ is the current winner. If $\text{cur} < P^\star = \min(M_{(1)}, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta)$, no opposing challenger exists, but Algorithm~\ref{alg:ladder} identifies $u_{(1)}$ as the sole remaining solvent candidate covering the decisive increment and places the final rung at $P^\star$, outbidding the runner-up valuation $M_{(2)}$. On the subsequent iteration, $\text{next}' > P^\star$ triggers loop termination.
  \item If $u_{(2)}$ placed the bid at $\text{cur} \leq M_{(2)}$, then $w = u_{(2)}$. Since $M_{(1)} \geq \text{next}$, $u_{(1)}$ is selected as challenger and places a bid at $\text{next} = \min(M_{(1)}, \text{cur} + \delta) = P^\star$, becoming winner. On the following iteration, $\text{next}' > P^\star$; $u_{(1)}$ is winner and all other candidates have been pruned. The loop terminates.
\end{enumerate}
In both cases, the ladder terminates with $u_{(1)}$ holding the winning bid at price $P^\star = \min(M_{(1)}, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta)$, establishing Vickrey equivalence across all arrival parities and grid alignments. When $M_{(1)} = M_{(2)}$, $P^\star = M_{(1)}$; the deterministic tie-break by registration timestamp ensures $u_{(1)}$ (earlier registrant) takes precedence and secures the winning bid.

The lock on the auction row ensures no concurrent PlaceBid can advance $\text{currentPrice}$ during the loop. If a concurrent PlaceBid was queued, it waits at the FOR UPDATE statement until our transaction commits, then re-reads $\text{currentPrice}$ and triggers a fresh ladder resolution -- a clean serial extension of the auction history.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Properties P3 and P4: Serializability and Deadlock Freedom}

\begin{theorem}[Serializability]
Any execution of ResolveAutoBids interleaved with other writers
(PlaceBid, Withdraw, EndAuction, ConfirmPayment) that obey the global
lock order is equivalent to a serial execution.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
All writers acquire the auction row lock before any wallet lock. The
auction row is the only object touched by every writer, so it acts as
the universal serialisation point: the order in which transactions
acquire the auction lock determines the serial schedule. Wallet locks
acquired second cannot create a cycle because the auction lock
strictly precedes them.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Deadlock Freedom]
No deadlock is possible between any two writers in the system that obey
the global lock order.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each transaction acquires locks in the same total order: first the
auction (single resource), then wallets in ascending \texttt{userId}.
By Gray and Reuter~\cite{Gray1992} \S7.4, lock ordering eliminates
wait-for cycles.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Property P5: Bounded Work and Complexity}

The exact number of ladder rungs executed by ResolveAutoBids is strictly
governed by the discrete clearing price:
\begin{equation}
  K^\star = \left\lceil \frac{P^\star - p_0}{\delta} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{\min(M_{(1)},\, \lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta) - p_0}{\delta} \right\rceil \leq \left\lceil \frac{\lfloor M_{(2)} \rfloor_\delta + \delta - p_0}{\delta} \right\rceil
  \label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
where $M_{(2)}$ is the second-highest limit in $\mathcal{P} \cup \{p_0\}$.
The ladder halts as soon as the runner-up cannot cover the next increment;
it never escalates to $M_{(1)}$ unless $M_{(2)} \geq M_{(1)} - \delta$. Even
in the worst-case configuration where $M_{(2)} = M_{(1)}$, the loop terminates
in $K_{\max} = \lceil (M_{(1)} - p_0)/\delta \rceil$ rungs.

Regarding computational complexity, each iteration performs $O(|\mathcal{P}|)$
in-memory candidate filtering and evaluation, and exactly $O(1)$ database writes
(inserting one bid row and updating two wallet records: decrementing the outbid
winner's hold and incrementing the challenger's hold). Across the full ladder,
the total database write volume is $O(K^\star)$ rows within a single atomic
transaction. For a typical English auction with $\delta = 1\%$ of $p_0$ and
$M_{(2)} \approx 1.5\times p_0$, $K^\star \approx 50$, well below the platform
safety ceiling ($10^5$).

% ─── VII. EVALUATION ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We compare two bid-processing pipelines on the same hardware
(Windows~11, Node.js~20, PostgreSQL~15, Redis~7) under identical
workloads driven by k6~\cite{k6}. Each run lasts 15~seconds against one
English auction priced at Rs.\,1{,}000 with $\delta =$~Rs.\,100. Wallet
balances are set high (Rs.\,100{,}000{,}000) so that running out of funds
never becomes a factor.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Direct (FOR UPDATE) --- Synchronous Persistence.}
    The Express endpoint handles requests synchronously at
    \texttt{POST /api/bids/auctions/:id}. It acquires the auction row lock
    \texttt{FOR UPDATE} inside a Prisma interactive transaction, enforces
    increment and wallet validation, updates current price and holds,
    inserts the bid row, and commits to the write-ahead log before returning
    HTTP~201. Completion is defined by durable database commit. Under
    concurrency, competing transactions queue in the PostgreSQL lock
    manager, accumulating lock wait time.

  \item \textbf{Sequencer (Redis Stream) --- Asynchronous Ingestion.}
    Bids arrive at \texttt{POST /api/bids/auctions/:id/stream} and are
    appended to a per-auction Redis Stream via \texttt{XADD}. The endpoint
    returns HTTP~202 (Accepted) immediately upon in-memory log receipt.
    Completion is defined by stream enqueue. A decoupled background consumer
    drains the stream via \texttt{XREADGROUP} and applies each bid to
    PostgreSQL serially using the Atomic Ladder transaction protocol.
    Because only a single worker writes to PostgreSQL, the database lock is
    never contested, but persistence commits lag enqueue by the consumer
    drain interval (measured at 150--200~bids/s persistence throughput).
\end{itemize}

% ─── VIII. DISCUSSION ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

\subsection{When Single-Jump Is Preferable}

The atomic ladder writes $K$ database rows where single-jump writes one.
If a platform treats the bid log as an internal ledger and not something
users read, as a high-frequency financial exchange would, those extra
writes are just overhead. The protocol is meant for consumer auction
sites that show a live, scrollable bid history, and it should be judged
in that setting.

\subsection{Anti-Sniping Interaction}

A ten-rung proxy ladder finishes in under a millisecond of wall-clock
time, but the manual bid that set it off may have landed inside the
auction's anti-sniping window. We extend the deadline once per manual
bid, not once per rung. That matches how a user thinks about it: the
platform is responding to one human action, not to ten separate events.

\subsection{Idempotency Under Job Re-Delivery}

BullMQ delivers at least once, so the ladder job can run twice. On the
second run the protocol sees that the price has already moved past every
challenger's ceiling and returns an empty step list. The outcome is not
byte-for-byte the same as the first run, since any manual bid that
arrived in between changes the starting point, but it is still correct in
practice: no duplicate rows are written and the price never goes
backwards.

\subsection{Multi-Node Distributed Architecture and Scalability}
\label{subsec:multinode}

Reviewers raised the question of how the Atomic Ladder Protocol behaves in
distributed, multi-node environments beyond our single-host benchmark
testbed. In a geographically distributed or multi-sharded deployment, two
key components require structural analysis:

\subsubsection{Distributed Relational Transactions (2PC and Consensus)}
When database state is horizontally partitioned across multiple nodes
(e.g., using CockroachDB, Google Spanner, or PostgreSQL with Citus), the
auction row $A$ and participating user wallets $\{W_u\}$ may reside on
different database shards. Executing ResolveAutoBids then necessitates a
distributed transaction coordinator employing Two-Phase Commit (2PC) over
Raft or Paxos consensus groups.

In such an environment, the two-tier lock order invariant (auction row first,
followed by wallets in ascending lexicographical \texttt{userId} order) remains
strictly sound: because the global lock order is universal, distributed
deadlocks across shards remain mathematically impossible. However, the commit
phase pays a consensus overhead of at least $2 \times \text{RTT}$ (prepare and
commit phases of 2PC) across participating shards, with additional replica consensus
rounds in Raft- or Paxos-backed stores. Under direct row-locking, this consensus delay amplifies
lock-hold duration, compounding lock contention when $C$ concurrent bidders
compete. To mitigate this in distributed relational backends, the iterative
ladder evaluation can be encapsulated within a database-side stored procedure
or stored Lua script, avoiding application-to-database network round-trips
during the inner loop.

\subsubsection{Partitioned Stream Sequencing (Kafka and Redis Cluster)}
In a horizontally scaled deployment, incoming bids are naturally partitioned
by auction identifier: routing bids to topics or streams partitioned by
$\text{hash}(\text{auctionId})$ guarantees that all bids and proxy ladder
triggers for auction $A$ route to a single partition leader.

Within each partition broker, a dedicated consumer worker maintains the
serialized, single-writer invariant against the partition's database shard.
Because all bids for the same auction are serialized at the message broker
partition prior to database entry, database row-lock contention is completely
eliminated. The sequencer absorbs traffic bursts at sub-millisecond network
ingress latency, while the consumer drains entries in serial batches into the
distributed store. Furthermore, scaling to thousands of concurrent auctions
is achieved trivially by adding partition brokers and worker instances, as
independent auctions have disjoint lock sets and require zero cross-partition
coordination.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

All empirical measurements were taken on a single host. In a multi-node setup
with network-separated primary-replica pairs and clustered Redis brokers,
network latency would increase absolute wall-clock times. However, because
network RTT amplifies synchronous lock-wait duration super-linearly under
contention (as predicted by queuing-theoretic models of lock waiting~\cite{Bernstein1987})
while affecting decoupled stream ingestion only additively, we
expect the relative throughput advantage of the sequenced pipeline over
direct row-locking to be even more pronounced in distributed networks.

We also did not test deliberate worker crashes at a chosen rung. Because
everything runs in one transaction, any such crash leaves no partial
state, so recoverability follows from the atomicity of {\tt COMMIT}
itself rather than from anything special in the protocol.

% ─── IX. CONCLUSION ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We have described the Atomic Ladder Protocol, which resolves concurrent
proxy bids in an English auction while keeping a complete per-increment
bid log. Every rung of the ladder runs inside a single database
transaction, the two-tier lock order rules out deadlocks, and the final
price matches the Vickrey equilibrium whatever order bidders arrive in.
We stated five properties and proved four of them formally (Theorems 1--4), establishing a closed-form iteration bound for P5.

On a PostgreSQL-and-Redis stack, the Redis Stream variant sustains
975~bids/s ingress throughput (and 182.4~bids/s durable persistence throughput, a $\mathbf{6.1\times}$ improvement
over the direct row-locking baseline), at an ingress 95th-percentile latency of
8.1~ms and with full log fidelity on every run. The protocol is
idempotent if a worker job is re-delivered, and it has been running in
the \textsc{AuctionHaus} platform since we deployed it.

Three things remain open: (i)~a cross-auction resolver that splits a
bidder's available balance across several proxy commitments at once;
(ii)~a machine-checked proof of the protocol in
TLA$^+$~\cite{Lamport2002}; and (iii)~empirical benchmarking under a live multi-node
distributed deployment (analyzed structurally in Section~\ref{subsec:multinode}).
