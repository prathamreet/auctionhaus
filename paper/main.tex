% ─── ABSTRACT ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Shill bidding -- the practice of placing fraudulent bids to inflate
auction prices artificially -- undermines the trust that buyers place in
online marketplaces. Published detection techniques uniformly operate on
completed auction logs, rendering real-time intervention impossible. This
paper presents an in-process fraud detection engine, integrated into the
\textsc{AuctionHaus} auction platform, that scores every bid event during
a live auction using five features derived from a sliding-window bid
graph. A logistic-regression classifier trained on these features flags
suspicious bidders before the auction closes, enabling administrators to
warn, throttle, or void bids while bidding is still active. Evaluation on
a purpose-built synthetic workload comprising four agent personas
(truthful, sniper, shill, and collusion ring) yields F1\,=\,0.927 at a
decision threshold of 0.05, compared to F1\,=\,0.000 for a na\"ive
outbid-count baseline. Feature ablation identifies reciprocity -- the
fraction of bidder-pairs engaged in mutual outbidding -- as the single
dominant signal; removing it collapses classification to chance level.
Additionally, this work contributes a SHA-256 commit-reveal protocol for
sealed-bid auctions and benchmarks a Redis Stream bid sequencer that
achieves a $\mathbf{32.5\times}$ throughput improvement over direct row locking at
100 concurrent bidders.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Auction systems, cryptographic commitments, fraud detection, graph
analytics, real-time systems, Redis Streams, shill bidding.

% ─── I. INTRODUCTION ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

Online auctions constitute a multi-billion dollar market segment in which
participant trust hinges on a single assumption: that competing bids
reflect genuine demand. {\em Shill bidding} violates this assumption. A
seller or confederate places bids with no intention of winning, driving
the price upward so that a legitimate buyer overpays. The practice is
prohibited by every major platform and illegal in many
jurisdictions~\cite{Trevathan2007}, yet it persists.

Detection efforts reported in the literature share a common limitation:
they are retrospective. Trevathan and Read~\cite{Trevathan2007} compute a
normalised shill score from completed auction histories. Ford, Xu, and
Valova~\cite{Ford2010} construct bidder-seller co-occurrence graphs over
finished auctions. Tsang, Koh, and Dobbie~\cite{Tsang2014} apply
community detection to post-hoc transaction dumps. None of these
approaches can act while an auction is still running.

We address this limitation with four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Online detection engine.} A sliding-window bid graph,
    maintained in memory and pruned lazily, from which five streaming
    features are extracted per bid event. A logistic-regression classifier
    scores each bid and emits {\tt fraud:flag} events to an
    administrative dashboard with sub-millisecond overhead.

  \item \textbf{Repeatable evaluation corpus.} A configurable agent
    simulator with four behavioural personas -- truthful, sniper, shill,
    and collusion ring -- that produces ground-truth-labelled bid logs
    for controlled experiments.

  \item \textbf{Sealed-bid privacy.} A SHA-256 commit-reveal protocol
    that provably conceals bid amounts from the server during sealed-bid
    auctions, satisfying both the hiding and binding properties without
    the computational cost of homomorphic encryption.

  \item \textbf{Throughput benchmarks.} A Redis Stream bid sequencer
    measured under load, demonstrating a $\mathbf{32.5\times}$ throughput gain at
    100 concurrent bidders relative to direct PostgreSQL row locking.
\end{enumerate}

% ─── II. RELATED WORK ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Shill-Bidding Detection}

Trevathan and Read~\cite{Trevathan2007} were the first to formalise the
shill detection problem for eBay-style English auctions, proposing a
composite shill score based on bid retraction rate, successive
outbidding, and winning-bid ratio. Their system requires a complete
auction history as input and cannot produce a score before the gavel
falls.

Ford, Xu, and Valova~\cite{Ford2010} broaden the feature space to
include bidder-seller co-occurrence graphs. On a labelled eBay dataset
they report precision of 0.84, yet their graph is rebuilt from scratch
after each auction closes -- a batch computation incompatible with live
intervention.

Tsang, Koh, and Dobbie~\cite{Tsang2014} employ community-detection
algorithms (modularity maximisation) over transaction graphs to identify
collusion rings. Their method requires at least three completed auctions
before a meaningful graph can be formed.

Our approach differs on two dimensions. First, features are computed over
a rolling time window rather than completed histories, so risk
accumulates gradually during bidding. Second, classification happens
in-process on every bid event, enabling the platform to act before the
auction closes.

\subsection{Cryptographic Auction Protocols}

Brandt~\cite{Brandt2006} surveys commitment schemes and homomorphic
encryption for sealed-bid auctions. Our protocol is a simplified
SHA-256 commit-reveal construction that achieves hiding and binding
without the overhead of homomorphic operations, at the cost of not
supporting range proofs -- a noted limitation discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:security}.

\subsection{Event-Sourced Systems}

Kleppmann~\cite{Kleppmann2017} describes event-sourcing architectures
using log-structured streams such as Apache Kafka. Our Redis Stream
sequencer applies the same per-partition ordering principle at a smaller
operational scale, using Redis Streams as the durable event log and a
consumer group for at-least-once delivery.

% ─── III. SYSTEM DESIGN ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{System Design}

\subsection{Platform Architecture}

\textsc{AuctionHaus} is implemented as a Node.js monorepo in TypeScript
and comprises four subsystems:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Backend:} Express~4, Socket.io~4, Prisma~5 (PostgreSQL~15),
    and BullMQ~5 (Redis~7) for job scheduling.
  \item \textbf{Frontend:} Next.js~16, React~19, TanStack~Query~5,
    Zustand~5, and Socket.io-client for bidirectional updates.
  \item \textbf{Fraud engine:} An in-process module described in
    Section~\ref{sec:fraud}.
  \item \textbf{Simulator:} A standalone TypeScript package at
    {\tt packages/simulator/} that drives evaluation workloads.
\end{itemize}

Three auction formats share a single {\tt Auction} model: English
(ascending bids with anti-snipe deadline extension and buy-now),
Dutch (automatic price drops via BullMQ recurring jobs), and
sealed-bid (private until close, with an optional commit-reveal phase).

\subsection{Financial Integrity}

All monetary values are stored as {\tt NUMERIC(18,2)} and handled as
Prisma {\tt Decimal} objects throughout the service layer, eliminating
the rounding errors inherent in IEEE-754 floating-point arithmetic. Bid
placement acquires the auction row via {\tt SELECT ... FOR UPDATE} and
then locks participating wallets in ascending {\tt userId} order,
following a global lock order that prevents deadlocks across all write
paths. Proxy-bid resolution runs atomically inside a single transaction,
writing every increment as a separate {\tt Bid} row.

% ─── IV. FRAUD DETECTION ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Fraud Detection Method}
\label{sec:fraud}

\subsection{Sliding-Window Bid Graph}

The engine maintains an in-memory directed graph
$\mathcal{G}(t, W)$ over a window of $W = 30$ minutes. Nodes represent
bidders and auctions; edges represent bid events labelled with amount and
timestamp. On each incoming bid, the graph is updated in $O(1)$
amortised time. Entries older than $t - W$ are evicted lazily during
insertion, keeping memory bounded.

\subsection{Feature Extraction}

For each bid event $e = (\text{bidder}, \text{auction}, \text{amount},
t)$, five features are computed from $\mathcal{G}$:

\textbf{F1 -- Response time ($\tau$).} Elapsed milliseconds between $e$
and the most recent bid by a different bidder on the same auction.
Sub-500\,ms responses suggest automated behaviour. The feature is set to
zero for the opening bid.

\textbf{F2 -- Bid frequency ($\phi$).} Total bids placed by this bidder
across all auctions within the window, normalised to bids per minute. An
elevated frequency is characteristic of accounts inflating multiple
auctions simultaneously.

\textbf{F3 -- Increment ratio ($\rho$).} The bid amount divided by
the auction's minimum increment. A value consistently near~1.0 across
consecutive bids points toward scripted minimum-increment driving.

\textbf{F4 -- Seller co-occurrence ($\sigma$).} The count of distinct
auctions created by the same seller in which this bidder has participated
within the window. Shill accounts affiliated with a particular seller
produce elevated co-occurrence scores.

\textbf{F5 -- Reciprocity ($\gamma$).} The fraction of bidder-pairs on
this auction that exhibit mutual outbidding. Values exceeding 0.5
indicate a collusion ring in which participants take turns raising the
price.

\subsection{Classification}

Feature vectors are z-scored using normalisation parameters derived from
50 simulator training runs and then scored by logistic regression:
\[
  P(\text{shill} \mid \mathbf{z}) = \sigma\!\left(\beta_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{5}
  \beta_i z_i\right), \quad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
\]

The weight vector $\beta = [-2.1, -1.8, 2.4, -1.6, 2.8, 3.2]$
(intercept, $\tau$, $\phi$, $\rho$, $\sigma$, $\gamma$) was derived by
grid search over the training corpus. Positive weights increase the fraud
score; the negative coefficient for $\tau$ reflects that {\em low} raw
response time is suspicious (the z-score normalisation inverts the
direction).

A bid is flagged when $P > \theta = 0.05$.

\subsection{Explainability}

Each flag is accompanied by a natural-language {\tt reason} string that
cites the specific features exceeding their respective thresholds (e.g.,
``response time 142\,ms (bot-speed); 4 auctions with same seller
(co-occurrence)''). This string is rendered inline on the administrative
fraud dashboard.

Ablation results confirm that reciprocity alone carries the
classification. Removing it reduces F1 to 0.000 -- the classifier
produces no true positives whatsoever. This is consistent with
intuition: mutual outbidding between the same pair of accounts is the
behavioural fingerprint of both shill and collusion strategies, and no
other single feature captures that pattern.

% ─── VI. SECURITY ANALYSIS ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Security Analysis}
\label{sec:security}

\subsection{Commit-Reveal Sealed Bids}

The sealed-bid protocol uses SHA-256 as the commitment function:
$H = \text{SHA-256}(\text{amountHex} \| \text{:} \| \text{nonce})$

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hiding.} SHA-256 is computationally indistinguishable
    from a random oracle; the commitment reveals nothing about the
    amount.
  \item \textbf{Binding.} Collision resistance ensures that no bidder
    can produce a distinct pair $(a', n')$ satisfying
    $H(a', n') = H(a, n)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitation.} The server cannot verify
$\text{amount} \geq \text{reservePrice}$ without learning the amount.
Full hiding with range verification requires Pedersen commitments
augmented by Bulletproof range proofs~\cite{Bunz2018}, which we defer to
future work.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

\textbf{Internal validity.} The simulator generates stylised agent
behaviour. Real shill operators may introduce timing jitter and variable
increment sizes. The classifier weights are hand-tuned; a larger labelled
corpus from a production deployment would improve calibration.

\textbf{External validity.} All benchmarks were collected on a single
machine. A geographically distributed deployment with network latency
would alter the absolute throughput figures, though the relative
comparison between pipelines is expected to hold.

\textbf{Construct validity.} F1 at a fixed threshold is sensitive to
class imbalance. In a production setting where shill bids are rare, a
precision-recall trade-off analysis (see Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}) is more
informative than a single F1 number.

% ─── VII. CONCLUSION ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We described an online shill-bidding detection engine that classifies
bid events in real time during live auctions. Five streaming features,
extracted from a sliding-window bid graph, feed a logistic-regression
classifier that achieves F1\,=\,0.927 on our synthetic evaluation
corpus -- a substantial improvement over the na\"ive outbid-count
baseline (F1\,=\,0.000). Ablation analysis confirms that reciprocity
is the dominant signal: removing it alone collapses classification to
chance. Combined with a cryptographic commit-reveal sealed-bid protocol
and a Redis Stream bid sequencer delivering a $\mathbf{32.5\times}$
throughput gain at high concurrency, the results suggest that practical
integrity mechanisms can be embedded in an auction platform without
measurable latency overhead.

Four directions for future work are identified: (a)~training the
classifier on a real-world labelled dataset from a production
deployment; (b)~replacing the SHA-256 commitment with Pedersen
commitments and Bulletproof range proofs; (c)~online weight adaptation
via stochastic gradient descent as new fraud patterns emerge; and
(d)~horizontal scaling evaluation of the Redis Stream consumer group
across multiple nodes.
